Project and-elim operands from shared tails of wide conjunctions

Pull request levnach/z3#47 into master · State: OPEN · Date: 2026-10-30T05:22:45Z · Loop iteration 47

This pull request is open and not merged. The change commit is ff890813c. The PR holds two commits on top of base 3b7a24739.

In one paragraph

A refutation from the legacy proof object often uses and-elimination to pull single operands out of a conjunction. The Lean reconstructor wrote each such step as a projection that walked from the very start of the right-nested conjunction. When a refutation eliminates every operand of a wide conjunction, the total work grows quadratically with its width. Lean then ran out of heartbeats and rejected the certificate, even though the proof was correct. This PR adds a let binding for the rest of the conjunction every 32 operands. Each and-elimination now starts from the nearest of these shared tails. A 600-operand implication chain that timed out now checks in about 6 seconds. The fix adds no axiom and no sorry. Lean still checks every step.

Background

Before this PR, small and medium Boolean refutations already reconstructed and checked. Earlier iterations taught the reconstructor to split big proofs into outlined lemmas, and to handle wide and deeply nested formulas. One gap was left open in the backlog from iteration 45. Refutations that eliminate most operands of a conjunction with 600 or more operands still failed in Lean. Iteration 45's 1,200-operand Real and Boolean chain, called mixed1200, failed the same way. This work serves milestone 2 of the plan, the kernel-checked Boolean slice. It also removes a scaling limit that the mixed Real and Boolean inputs of milestone 3 ran into.

What changed

  • Lean can now check refutations that eliminate hundreds of operands from one wide conjunction, because no projection walks more than 32 levels from its starting point.
  • The reconstructor now emits one shared tail binding per 32 operands, so a conjunction's prefix is walked once per lemma instead of once per operand.
  • Outlined lemmas and the single main theorem both place the tails right after the step that proves the conjunction, whether that step lives inside the lemma or comes from another lemma.
  • Three new Lean-checked tests guard the 600-operand outlined case, a 150-operand inline case, and an 80-operand case where the conjunction's proof sits inside the eliminating lemma.
  • The plan document gained a dated note that records the problem, the fix, and the new tests.

An analogy helps. Before, finding page 500 of a book meant counting from page 1 every time. Now there is a bookmark every 32 pages, and each lookup starts from the closest bookmark. A premise that depends on a hypothesis still uses the old direct projection. Its tails would need those hypotheses too, and no input has needed this yet.

Review

The reviewer approved the change. There were no blockers and no major findings.

  • Minor: an untested code path. The reviewer looked for a test where the conjunction's proof sits inside the same outlined lemma that eliminates from it. None existed. The tails go after that local proof step, in a separate branch of the code. A future edit could place them too early, and Lean would then fail with an unknown identifier while CI stayed green. The reviewer checked the branch by hand and found it correct today. Fixed: a new 80-operand test forces outlining and checks the result in Lean. It also checks that the first tail comes after the local proof step.
  • Nit: a test pattern that matched too little. The test's regular expression was meant to bound every projection's length. It never matched a projection that starts from a tail, because of how tail names are spelled. So a projection that ran too long from a tail would have slipped through. Fixed: tails and projections are now matched separately. Each tail must skip exactly 32 operands, and each projection at most 31.
  • Nit: an overstated doc note. The note said every conjunction of 600 or more operands failed. In fact only refutations that eliminate most of the operands failed. It also pointed to a case that the cited section does not name. Fixed: the note was reworded and now names mixed1200.
  • Suggestion: skip tails when there is only one and-elimination. Not done. The total number of projections is the same either way, so it costs nothing measurable.

The reviewer also tried tampering with the output. Changing one tail to skip 31 operands instead of 32 made Lean report a type mismatch. The tails are therefore checked by Lean, not trusted.

Risks and open questions

  • The generated Lean text changes for every and-elimination at operand position 32 or later. This includes older wide tests and clause log certificates. The full suite passes.
  • Each outlined lemma rebinds the tails it needs. That adds about one binding per 32 operands per lemma.
  • Many let bindings in one declaration cost Lean more than linear time. A synthetic test with 1,200 bindings took far longer than one with 500. Lemma chunks stay at 500 steps, so the pipeline avoids this, but it limits how large chunks can grow.
  • Premises that depend on hypotheses still project from the start. Whether real inputs hit this is unknown.

What comes next

  • Other helpers in the reconstructor build long And.left and And.right chains in the same quadratic way. These include the prover, the def-axiom lemmas, and the EUF terms.
  • That pattern may be related to the kernel memory blowup on the tta_startup def-axiom case, which still does not verify.
  • The quadratic assume equivalence checks on the FISCHER inputs remain open from iteration 45.

Glossary

and-elimination (and-elim)
A proof rule. From a proof of A and B and C, it concludes one operand, such as B.
axiom
A statement accepted without proof. A trustworthy certificate should add no new ones.
Boolean
The sort of truth values, true and false. In SMT-LIB it is written Bool.
certificate
The proof data Z3 exports so that another tool can check the result on its own.
clause log
The newer of Z3's two proof sources, a list of clauses the solver derived, each with a hint for checking it.
conjunction
An "and" of several formulas. (and p q r) is true only when all three are true.
def-axiom
A proof rule that states a basic fact about a Boolean connective, such as "if A and B holds, then A holds".
EUF
Equality with uninterpreted functions, the theory that reasons about = and congruence.
heartbeat
Lean's unit of work. A declaration that uses more than its budget, here 200,000, is rejected with a timeout.
hypothesis
A temporary assumption inside a proof, later discharged. A step that depends on one is valid only under it.
implication
"If A then B", written (=> A B) in SMT-LIB and A -> B in Lean.
isDefEq
Lean's check that two expressions mean the same thing after unfolding definitions. Long projection chains make it slow.
kernel
The small core of Lean that checks every finished proof term. It is the part one has to trust.
legacy proof object
The older of Z3's two proof sources, a tree of named proof rules. The other source is the clause log.
lemma
A smaller theorem proved on its own and then used inside a bigger proof.
let binding
Giving an expression a name so later lines can reuse it. let t := x.2.2 lets you write t.1 later.
operand
One of the parts a connective joins. In (and p q r), the operands are p, q and r.
outlined lemma
A slice of a large proof that the reconstructor moves into its own lemma, so Lean checks it separately.
premise
A fact that a proof step takes as input. The premise of an and-elimination is the conjunction.
projection
Taking one part of a pair. In Lean, h.1 is the left part of a proof of A ∧ B, and h.2 is the right part.
quadratic
Growing with the square of the size. Doubling the width roughly quadruples the work.
refutation
A proof that a set of formulas leads to false, so they cannot all hold at once.
right-nested
A shape where each "and" holds one operand on the left and the rest on the right: A ∧ (B ∧ (C ∧ D)). Reaching D takes three steps to the right.
sorry
A Lean placeholder that skips a proof. A finished certificate must not contain it.
sort
The type of a value, such as Int, Real, or Bool.
